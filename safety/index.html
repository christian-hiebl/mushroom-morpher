Safety and limitations

What this website does, what it cannot do, and why no comparison of shape can identify a mushroom or tell you whether it is safe to eat.

Never eat a wild mushroom because of anything this website shows. Mushroom Morpher is an educational toy. It does not identify mushrooms and it gives no advice on toxicity or edibility. If someone may have eaten a poisonous mushroom, see the emergency section.

What the tool does

You shape a stylised 3D mushroom: cap shape and size, stem length and width, colour, the type of spore-bearing surface, and a few other traits. The site compares those choices with the traits recorded for 1,000 species and shows the ones whose recorded traits are most similar. You can also pick a species by name and have a model built from its recorded traits.

The species data comes from English Wikipedia: the structured morphology box on each species article, measurements and colours read from the article text, and the lead photo. It is a way to explore how varied mushroom forms are, and nothing more.

What it does not do

  • It does not identify anything. It never examines a real specimen. It compares a drawing you made with a short list of recorded traits.
  • It does not analyse photographs. The photos shown are illustrations taken from Wikipedia articles.
  • It does not assess toxicity or edibility. Labels such as "edible" are deliberately not shown. A species shown without a warning label is not a statement that it is safe.
  • It does not cover the fungi near you. The 1,000 species are the ones with the most-read Wikipedia articles. That is a small fraction of the many thousands of described mushroom-forming species, chosen by popularity and not by region. The mushroom in front of you may simply not be in the data.
  • It does not express confidence. The order of the reference species reflects similarity of a few recorded traits. It is not a probability that anything is that species.

Limits of comparing by shape

  • The traits are coarse. A cap is described with a handful of words such as "convex" or "flat". Many unrelated species share exactly the same description.
  • The data is incomplete. Hundreds of the species have no recorded cap or stem size, and about half have no recorded colour. Some colours were extracted automatically from article text and are sometimes wrong.
  • The source can be wrong. Wikipedia articles vary in quality and can be edited by anyone.
  • Real mushrooms vary. One species can look very different depending on age, weather and where it grows. Caps open from rounded to flat, colours fade or wash out in rain, and surface features such as the white warts of the fly agaric can be washed off entirely.
  • The model is idealised. A smooth symmetrical 3D shape leaves out texture, smell, damage, and everything else a real specimen shows.

Why identification needs more than appearance

People who identify mushrooms reliably work with the whole fresh specimen and with features this tool cannot represent, including:

  • the base of the stem, which must be dug up intact. The sac-like cup at the base of the death cap is often hidden below ground, and it is lost when a mushroom is cut off at the surface;
  • spore print colour, taken from the actual specimen;
  • smell, texture, and how the flesh changes colour when cut or bruised;
  • whether the mushroom releases a milky fluid, and what colour it is;
  • what it grows on and which trees are nearby, since many species live only with particular hosts;
  • microscopic features such as the size, shape and ornamentation of the spores;
  • chemical spot tests and, increasingly, DNA sequencing.

Some species can only be told apart under a microscope or by DNA. No amount of care with shape and colour can separate them.

Photographs and shape alone can be insufficient

A photograph usually shows one angle of one specimen. It can hide the underside of the cap and the base of the stem, it distorts colour depending on light and camera, and it carries no smell, texture or sense of scale. Studies that tested photo-identification apps have found that they misidentify dangerous species, and poison centres have warned against relying on them. A shape comparison such as this one uses far less information than a photograph does.

Toxicity cannot be inferred from appearance

There is no visual sign of a poisonous mushroom. Deadly species can be plain, attractive, pleasant-smelling and, according to people who survived eating them, pleasant-tasting. All of the traditional rules are false, including these:

  • "Poisonous mushrooms tarnish silver."
  • "If the cap peels, it is safe."
  • "If animals or insects eat it, it is safe for people."
  • "Mushrooms growing on wood are safe."
  • "Cooking, drying or boiling removes the poison."

The toxins responsible for most fatal poisonings, the amatoxins, are not destroyed by cooking. Symptoms typically begin many hours after the meal, often after a deceptive period of feeling fine, and by then serious liver damage may already be under way. Other species are harmful only when eaten raw or together with alcohol, and people can react differently to the same mushroom.

Regional differences matter

Which species occur, and which dangerous lookalikes occur alongside them, depends on where you are. Knowledge from one region does not transfer safely to another. A well-documented cause of fatal poisonings is people collecting death caps in a new country because they resemble an edible species they knew at home, such as the paddy straw mushroom of Southeast Asia.

Names are not reliable across regions either. DNA studies have shown that many mushrooms long treated as one species across Europe and North America are in fact several different species. A field guide written for another continent can therefore mislead. This site records where a species occurs only at the level of continents, which is far too coarse to say what grows in a particular forest.

Lookalike species exist

Many edible mushrooms have toxic lookalikes, some of them deadly. Well-known examples:

  • The death cap (Amanita phalloides) and the destroying angels have been mistaken for paddy straw mushrooms, field mushrooms, green brittlegills and, when young and still enclosed, puffballs.
  • The funeral bell (Galerina marginata) contains the same toxins as the death cap and resembles several small brown edible mushrooms that grow on wood.
  • False morels (Gyromitra) are confused with true morels.
  • Jack-o'-lantern mushrooms (Omphalotus) are confused with chanterelles.
  • The green-spored parasol (Chlorophyllum molybdites) is confused with edible parasols and is a leading cause of mushroom poisoning in North America.

In several of these pairs the differences lie in features this tool does not model at all. Two species can also appear side by side here as "similar-looking" precisely because they are lookalikes.

For real identification, consult a qualified local expert

If you want to know what a mushroom is, and above all if you are thinking of eating it, have the actual specimen examined in person by someone qualified who knows the fungi of your area. Local mycological societies run identification sessions, and some countries have official mushroom advisory or inspection services. Bring the whole mushroom including the base of the stem, ideally several specimens of different ages, and note where and on what it was growing.

A website, an app, a photo posted online, or a book on its own is not a substitute.

If someone may have eaten a poisonous mushroom

Contact your local poison control centre or emergency services immediately. Do not wait for symptoms: with the most dangerous species they appear late, and early treatment matters. Keep any remaining mushrooms, leftovers of the meal and photographs, because they help experts work out what was eaten.
